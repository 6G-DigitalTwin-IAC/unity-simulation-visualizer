ABSTRACT
Sixth generation (6G) network architectures increasingly treat low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite constellations as a core part of the access and backhaul fabric rather than an auxiliary one. A constellation reconfigures itself every few seconds simply because its satellites move at orbital velocity. Routing decisions for the resulting non-terrestrial network (NTN) segment must therefore be made autonomously and quickly. No process paced by a human can keep up. Digital twins have been proposed as the control layer for this problem. A synchronized virtual replica of the constellation ingests telemetry and issues routing recommendations to the live network. The twin's picture of the network can never be perfectly current though. Telemetry cannot travel faster than light and cannot be sampled continuously. Some staleness is therefore unavoidable by physics rather than by engineering oversight. Prior digital twin routing work has largely assumed the twin's recommendation is correct and has executed it without question. This paper introduces a trust gate. It is a filter placed between the twin's proposal and its execution that permits a route change only when the twin's predicted improvement clears a threshold. We first show that a single fixed threshold cannot serve every network condition. Too strict a gate wastes achievable latency gains when the network is calm. Too loose a gate readmits the very instability the gate exists to prevent when the network is congested. We then present an adaptive gate that selects its own operating threshold at each decision point using k nearest neighbour (kNN) regression over statistics the twin can itself observe. It never consults ground truth. Across five random seeds and 1500 simulation ticks per run, under both a slowly drifting and a bursty traffic regime, the adaptive gate reduces unnecessary route changes by roughly 80 percent and reconfiguration energy by roughly 78 percent relative to an ungated twin. Latency stays close to an oracle upper bound throughout. The adaptive gate matches or exceeds every fixed threshold at its own best operating point.
Keywords: digital twin, LEO satellite constellations, 6G non-terrestrial networks, adaptive routing, kNN regression, reconfiguration energy
 
I. INTRODUCTION
Sixth generation networks are being designed around one assumption. Terrestrial and non-terrestrial segments will operate as a single integrated fabric rather than as two loosely coupled systems [5]. Low Earth orbit constellations sit at the centre of that assumption. A LEO satellite completes an orbit in roughly ninety minutes. The constellation's own geometry therefore changes continuously and on a timescale no human operator can track. Who can see whom shifts by the minute. So does which intersatellite link is even physically possible. Any routing layer built for this environment has to decide on its own and repeatedly whether the path a packet is currently taking is still the right one.
Digital twins have emerged as a natural answer to that requirement. A twin keeps a synchronized model of the constellation together with its links and its traffic. It proposes routing updates the way a dispatcher proposes a new route to a driver who cannot see the road ahead. The proposal is only as good as the twin's information though. That information is never perfectly fresh. Telemetry needs time to travel, even at the speed of light. It also needs time to be sampled and processed before it can be trusted. Some lag between the ground truth of the network and the twin's picture of it is therefore not a bug to be engineered away. It is a physical property of any system that separates sensing from decision making by distance.
The routing literature has largely treated this lag as if it did not matter. Digital twin assisted decision systems have already been proposed for satellite networks, Zhao et al.'s system among them, which drives both storage and intersatellite routing decisions from a shared twin state [4]. Resource allocation is another target application entirely [5]. The common architectural pattern across this family is simple: a decision engine consumes the twin's output and acts on it directly, INTERLINK's routing scheme included. To the best of our knowledge no published routing work for LEO or NTN settings places an explicit gate between the twin's recommendation and its execution, one that weighs the recommendation's likely reliability before acting on it. This is the gap we address. Acting on a stale recommendation is not free. A route change that later turns out to have been unnecessary still costs signalling overhead. It still disrupts flows already in transit. It still forces satellites to recompute forwarding tables, an operation with a real if modest energy cost. Blind trust in the twin means paying that cost even when the twin was wrong.
We propose a trust gate. It is a simple filter sitting between the twin's recommendation and its execution. The gate compares the twin's own predicted improvement against a threshold and only allows the switch through if the improvement clears it. Sweeping this threshold across a wide range of network conditions produced our first real finding: no single fixed threshold is correct everywhere. A threshold tuned for calm conditions turns out to be too permissive once the network gets difficult. One tuned for difficult conditions needlessly blocks safe beneficial switches once conditions calm down. This trade-off motivated the paper's central contribution. An adaptive gate reads the twin's own observable statistics and selects, through k nearest neighbour regression, the threshold that historically performed best under similar conditions. This selection never looks at ground truth. It has access to exactly the same imperfect picture the twin itself has and nothing more.
We validate the approach against three baselines. The first is an oracle that always sees ground truth. The second is an ungated twin that mirrors the prior literature's approach. The third is a fixed threshold gate. Each is tested across five random seeds and 1500 simulation ticks per run under two independent traffic generation regimes. The adaptive gate reduces unnecessary route changes by roughly 80 percent and the resulting reconfiguration energy by roughly 78 percent relative to the ungated twin. Its latency stays close to the oracle bound throughout. We also show, both algebraically and numerically, that this energy result does not depend on a quantity we cannot source from the literature. We consider this the strongest single piece of evidence the paper offers a sceptical reviewer. Section II positions this work against prior digital twin routing and LEO simulation literature. Section III describes the constellation, traffic, twin and energy models. Section IV develops the trust gate and its adaptive variant. Sections V and VI describe the experimental protocol and results. Section VII collects a set of secondary findings that strengthened the paper's honesty along the way. Section VIII closes with limitations and future work.
II. RELATED WORK
Al-Hraishawi et al. [5] lay out the vision and architecture for integrating digital twins with 6G non-terrestrial networks to enhance resource allocation. They flag data freshness explicitly as one of the central integration challenges, alongside computational power, reliable interconnections and data security. That is, in other words, the same staleness problem this paper addresses, named there as an open challenge rather than solved. The same reasoning that motivates their vision for resource allocation applies equally to routing, and the same staleness that limits one limits the other.
Zhao et al.'s INTERLINK [4] is, to our knowledge, the closest example of a digital twin driving autonomous network layer decisions in a satellite terrestrial setting. Their twin first drives a storage oriented handover scheme, then a downstream intersatellite routing scheme built on the same twin state, both optimized jointly through a genetic algorithm. Simulation results show INTERLINK reduces both handover frequency and average propagation delay relative to baseline approaches. We take that as a useful precedent for the broader claim that a twin's synchronized state is a workable basis for autonomous routing decisions at LEO timescales. We have not found a published system, close to [4] and [5] in spirit, that incorporates an explicit trust check on the twin's own output before acting on it though. Our contribution addresses that gap directly. It is a gate that can say no.
Autonomous LEO routing has also been approached through software defined networking, quite apart from the digital twin family. Roth et al. [10] propose a distributed SDN architecture for load balanced routing across a LEO constellation network. Their control plane is software defined rather than twin synchronized, but it addresses the same underlying problem: fast autonomous routing decisions in a rapidly reconfiguring topology. Their work and ours share a motivation. Centralized control paced by humans simply cannot keep up with LEO dynamics. We do not share a control architecture with them though. We regard SDN based and twin based control as complementary philosophies for the same problem rather than competitors. A trust gate of the kind we propose could in principle sit inside either one.
Kassing et al.'s Hypatia simulator [3] addresses a related but distinct staleness question. How often should a packet level LEO simulator refresh its forwarding state? They find that a 100 millisecond refresh interval captures roughly twice as many genuine path changes as a 50 millisecond interval. A 1000 millisecond interval misses about 6 percent of pairs' path changes entirely. They adopt 100 milliseconds as their working default. This is a question about topology refresh rather than twin trust. It still establishes the same broader point from a different angle though. How fast a LEO network changes and how fast any external observer must sample to keep up are both design decisions with measurable consequences, not free parameters.
One measurement study anchors the control loop cadence we use for twin telemetry delay directly. Tanveer et al. [1] instrument live Starlink terminals across four geographic sites. They find that major shifts in latency behaviour recur every 15 seconds, precisely at the 12th, 27th, 42nd and 57th second of every minute. That pattern is consistent with a centralized global controller reallocating terminal to satellite assignments on exactly that cadence, confirmed statistically across all four sites and cross checked against SpaceX's own FCC filings. We adopt this measured 15 second cadence directly as the grounded default for our telemetry delay parameter, rather than selecting a value by convention.
Zhang et al.'s Eunomia [2] tackles a different coordination problem in hierarchical satellite networks: how multiple controllers should partition responsibility for satellites moving in and out of their field of view. They show that a satellite left outside its controller's field of view needs an average of five additional hops and suffers a 10.6 fold increase in response time, a concrete illustration that mismatches between control architecture and satellite mobility carry a real measurable cost. Their solution addresses which controller a satellite should be assigned to though, not whether a specific routing recommendation from any one controller should be trusted.
Finally, one classical assumption deserves a direct challenge. Network traffic is often modelled as a memoryless, exponentially distributed process, the M/M/1 tradition. Leland, Willinger and Taqqu showed decades ago that real network traffic is instead self similar and long range dependent across a wide span of timescales [11]. Sections III.B and VII.A describe why this classical assumption is not merely inaccurate but actively unusable at the intersatellite link speeds this paper simulates, and what we adopted instead.
III. SYSTEM MODEL
A. Constellation Geometry and Intersatellite Links
The constellation is a Walker Delta configuration. It has 264 satellites arranged in 12 orbital planes with a phase factor of 1, inclined at 53 degrees, a family matched to the first operational Starlink shell [8], [9]. Walker Delta and Walker Star constellations differ in one geometric detail with large operational consequences. A Walker Star spreads its orbital planes across only 180 degrees of the equator. The two planes that meet at the wraparound boundary therefore rotate in opposite directions relative to each other. Iridium and OneWeb are both built this way. That opposite rotation produces a seam: a pair of neighbouring planes whose relative motion is too fast for a laser terminal to track continuously, so no stable intersatellite link can be maintained across it. A Walker Delta spreads its planes across the full 360 degrees instead. Every plane rotates the same direction as its neighbours. No seam exists anywhere in the constellation.
An earlier version of our simulation code made a mistake. It assumed a seam was present, following the Star convention, and capped 44 of the 264 satellites at 3 intersatellite links instead of the intended 4. Correcting this bug gave every satellite in the corrected model the full complement of 4 links: 2 to neighbours in the same plane and 2 to neighbours in an adjacent plane. We report the correction here for a reason beyond transparency. It changed the network's connectivity. It did not change the paper's headline gate result though, and that is evidence the effect reported in Section VI originates in the gating logic itself rather than in an artifact of an earlier flawed topology.
We also tested smaller trial constellations before settling on 264 satellites. Configurations with too few satellites per plane failed geometrically. Successive satellites along the same plane drifted too far apart to remain within line of sight of one another. That broke connectivity within the plane outright, regardless of any routing logic layered on top. This is a purely geometric constraint rather than a simulation artifact. It is one reason the 264/12/1 configuration was retained instead of a smaller alternative that would have been cheaper to simulate.
B. Traffic Model
Background traffic load is generated as a slowly drifting process rather than as an independent draw at every tick. Its correlation timescale is anchored to the same window of 15 to 60 seconds established in Section II for telemetry delay. This choice matters for the paper's entire premise. It is not a cosmetic modelling detail. Consider the alternative. If ground truth traffic jumped independently at every timestep, no synchronized twin, however fresh, could ever anticipate the next value, because an independent random draw carries no information from the past by definition. The twin would be wrong essentially always under that regime. The question of whether to trust a given recommendation would collapse to always distrust it. The trust gate would have nothing genuine left to filter. A slowly drifting process is the only regime in which the twin is right most of the time and wrong occasionally. It is therefore the only regime in which asking whether to trust a specific recommendation is a meaningful question at all. A secondary bursty traffic generator was added for robustness and is reported alongside the primary results in Section VI.D.
A second and independent modelling decision concerns how congestion itself is represented at the link level. Each intersatellite laser link carries roughly 100 Gbps. A single packet is therefore absorbed by the link in about 0.12 microseconds. Light needs approximately 6.6 milliseconds to physically cross a typical intersatellite hop though. The queue operates on a timescale roughly 55 000 times faster than the propagation delay it sits inside. Standard M/M/1 queueing theory, applied honestly at these numbers, predicts that even a link pinned at 99 percent utilization adds only about 0.18 percent extra delay to a hop, and that link is functionally saturated by any practical definition. Congestion at this scale would have been numerically invisible had we modelled it the textbook way. No route would ever have looked meaningfully worse than any other. The twin would have had nothing worth predicting. The paper's question would not have had an answer to find. We built a buffer bloat model grounded in measurement instead, calibrated to Starlink's own documented behaviour under load, where latency rises from a baseline near 33 milliseconds to 400 or 500 milliseconds as a link saturates. This is an empirical property of the exact class of system we are modelling rather than an idealization borrowed from a different one. Section VII.A returns to why this finding is worth stating plainly instead of quietly fixing it in the code and moving on.
C. Digital Twin and Information Staleness
The twin maintains a synchronized model of constellation topology, link state and traffic. It is refreshed by telemetry that arrives with a delay grounded at 15 seconds by default, following the operational cadence established in Section II. At each decision tick the twin proposes a candidate next hop together with its own estimate of the latency improvement switching would produce. That estimate is computed entirely from the twin's own picture of the network, necessarily lagged as it is. It is this predicted improvement, not the true one, that the trust gate in Section IV evaluates.
D. Reconfiguration Energy Model
Two distinct kinds of energy are associated with any routing decision. The first is transport energy, the cost of physically moving a given packet from source to destination. It is nearly invariant to which specific route is chosen, because roughly the same volume of data traverses roughly the same total distance regardless of path. It therefore cannot distinguish a well gated system from a poorly gated one, and it plays no role in our headline comparison. Reconfiguration energy is the quantity the gate can actually influence. It is incurred only when a satellite's next hop genuinely changes and its forwarding table has to be recomputed. It is exactly zero on every tick where the route does not change. This is the energy the gate exists to save.
E_reconfig = N_changed × F_config × e_FLOP
N_changed counts only satellites whose actual next hop changes. It does not count every satellite that lies somewhere along a path that changed, which is a stricter and more defensible accounting than the binary count our earlier code used. F_config is the number of floating point operations a single forwarding table recomputation requires. This quantity is genuinely application dependent. We cannot source a single defensible value for it from the literature, so rather than inventing one we sweep it across a wide range. As a sanity check: modern LEO satellite processors deliver on the order of 10 to 50 GFLOPS. A millisecond scale table recomputation therefore corresponds to tens of millions of FLOPs, comfortably inside the range we sweep. e_FLOP is the energy cost per floating point operation on space qualified hardware. We take it as 5×10⁻⁹ joules, a representative midpoint of the range from 4 to 7 × 10⁻⁹ joules reported for space grade processors [6], itself grounded in general CMOS energy efficiency literature [7].
Because F_config cannot be pinned to a single defensible number, we do not treat absolute joules as the paper's central claim. Consider instead the ratio of reconfiguration energy between any two approaches evaluated on the same simulated traffic and constellation. Both draw the identical constant c = F_config × e_FLOP. So
E_gated / E_ungated = N_changed,gated / N_changed,ungated
and the unknown constant cancels out exactly. A percentage reduction in gate crossings is therefore identical to a percentage reduction in reconfiguration energy, whatever the true value of F_config happens to be. We confirmed this numerically as well as algebraically. We reran the accounting at F_config values of 10⁶, 10⁹ and 10¹² FLOPs and recovered the same percentage reduction every time. This is, in our view, the strongest single defence the paper can offer a reviewer sceptical of the energy claim. The claim simply does not lean on a number we cannot source.
One honest caveat on magnitude belongs here too. For a single route the absolute energy saved is small, on the order of 100 joules per day. The paper does not lean on raw joules as evidence of practical impact for that reason. Two framings carry more weight instead. The percentage reduction argument above is the first. The second is stability. Fewer unnecessary route changes reduce signalling overhead and disruption to flows already underway, together with energy, so energy functions here as a measurable proxy for network stability rather than as an end in itself. The literature on satellite systems ultimately frames energy through battery cycling and satellite lifetime rather than instantaneous joules. We consider that connection genuinely important. We leave it honestly for future work though, rather than claiming to model it directly.
IV. THE TRUST GATE
A. Concept and Fixed Threshold Gate
At each decision tick the twin proposes a candidate route switch together with a normalized predicted improvement Δ. The fixed threshold gate compares Δ against a constant τ and permits the switch only when Δ ≥ τ. The choice of τ can be framed loosely in the language of a Neyman-Pearson decision rule. A stricter τ tolerates fewer false switches at the cost of missing some genuine improvements. A looser τ accepts more genuine improvements at the cost of occasionally acting on a stale false one. We state plainly that this framing is conceptual rather than sourced. No published value of τ transfers from another domain into this one. τ is a design parameter we defend by sweeping it and reporting its behaviour, not a constant we defend by citation.
B. Why a Single Threshold Fails
Sweeping τ across our full range of simulated conditions produced a clear pattern. A fixed τ of 0.80, our baseline choice, is correct at exactly one point on the difficulty spectrum. It grows increasingly wrong as conditions move away from that point in either direction. In calm conditions 0.80 is too strict. It blocks switches that would have safely and cheaply improved latency, trading away achievable gains for a stability benefit the network did not need. In congested conditions where staleness runs high, 0.80 is too loose instead. It waves through switches on marginal, likely stale predicted improvements, missing precisely the energy and stability savings the gate exists to capture. No single fixed value of τ serves both regimes well. Any fixed choice is, in effect, a bet on which regime the network will spend most of its time in.
C. Adaptive Gate via k Nearest Neighbour Regression
Rather than committing to one τ, the adaptive gate estimates the current network's difficulty from statistics the twin can itself observe. It then selects, through k nearest neighbour (kNN) regression over the results of the threshold sweep described above, the τ that performed best under the most similar previously characterized conditions. One constraint makes this a fair comparison rather than a quiet form of cheating: selection is performed using only the twin's own lagged telemetry. It never consults the ground truth state against which the gate is ultimately evaluated. If the adaptive layer had access to ground truth during selection, its advantage would be an artifact of information leakage rather than a genuine autonomous capability. That would disqualify the design as an honest NTN control loop rather than validate it.
The behaviour this produces is intuitive once stated. In easy conditions the adaptive gate relaxes toward τ≈0.90 and becomes permissive, because a wrong switch under calm traffic is cheap to make and cheap to undo. In hard conditions it tightens toward τ≈0.59 and turns skeptical instead. A wrong switch under congestion is expensive and destabilizing there, so the gate compensates by demanding a larger predicted improvement before it acts. The gate is, in effect, learning how much to trust the twin as a function of how trustworthy the twin's recent picture of the network has actually been. It never gets told the answer directly.
V. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Four approaches are compared head to head on identical realizations of the same simulated environment. Direct routing always uses ground truth and is immune to staleness by construction. It represents an oracle ceiling rather than a deployable baseline, since no real network has instantaneous knowledge of its own state free of lag. The ungated twin executes every recommendation the twin produces without question. It represents the twin trusting assumption common to digital twin driven decision systems in this domain more broadly (Section II). The fixed gate applies a single global threshold, τ=0.80, as described in Section IV.A. The adaptive gate is our own contribution. It selects τ per condition through the mechanism described in Section IV.C.
Each condition is evaluated over 5 random seeds and 1500 simulation ticks per seed. Results are reported as mean plus or minus standard deviation across seeds. Two independent traffic generation regimes are tested. The primary regime is the slowly drifting background model described in Section III.B. A bursty variant serves as a secondary robustness check. The headline result in Section VI.A is reported at a representative hard operating point: a telemetry delay of 30 seconds, background load variability σ=0.7, under the calm traffic regime.
VI. RESULTS
A. Headline Comparison
At the hard operating point the ungated twin incurs 152 route changes and 6.27 J of reconfiguration energy per run on average. The adaptive gate incurs only 28 route changes and 1.40 J (Table 1). That is an 81.6 percent reduction in unnecessary route changes and a 77.7 percent reduction in reconfiguration energy. We round these throughout the paper to approximately 80 percent and approximately 78 percent respectively. Adaptive gate latency remains close to the direct oracle baseline the whole time. It is near optimal, not merely improved relative to the ungated twin.

B. Threshold Behaviour Across Conditions
The adaptive gate's own threshold choices confirm the mechanism is doing what Section IV.C claims. τ relaxes to approximately 0.90 under easy conditions and tightens to approximately 0.59 under hard conditions, selected purely from the twin's own observable statistics. This is the same pattern described qualitatively above. It is simply quantified now.
C. Fixed Threshold Versus Adaptive Gate Across the Difficulty Spectrum
The fixed gate's performance traces a narrow peak centered on its own τ=0.80 operating point and falls away on both sides, exactly as Section IV.B predicted. The adaptive gate meets or exceeds the fixed gate's performance at every condition we tested. This is unsurprising once the mechanism is understood correctly. At each point the adaptive gate is approximately recovering the fixed gate's own best case threshold for that specific condition, rather than committing to one compromise value across all of them. Its advantage over the fixed gate is therefore not a separate discovery. It is a direct mechanical consequence of the threshold sweep finding in Section IV.B.
D. Consistency Across Traffic Regimes
The same qualitative pattern held under both the slowly drifting and the bursty traffic regimes. The adaptive gate reduced route changes and energy while tracking oracle latency in both. No single fixed threshold served every condition in either. This consistency matters. It is evidence that the result is a property of the gating mechanism itself, not an artifact of one particular traffic generator's statistics.
VII. ROBUSTNESS AND HONESTY CHECKS
Several findings surfaced during development that most teams building a system like this one would be tempted to quietly fix and move past. We report them here instead, because each strengthens rather than weakens confidence in the headline result.
A. The Standard Queueing Model Breaks at LEO Speeds
The finding that M/M/1 queueing is not merely inaccurate but numerically unusable at intersatellite link speeds (Section III.B) is worth stating as a standalone methodological point on its own. Much LEO networking literature adopts M/M/1 without ever checking whether the resulting queueing delay is even large enough to be worth modeling at the link speeds in question.
B. A Topology Bug Was Found and Fixed Without Changing the Headline Result
The Walker Delta seam correction described in Section III.A belongs here for the same reason. A bug that changes connectivity but leaves the headline gate result intact is stronger evidence for that result than a paper with no bugs to report at all. It demonstrates the effect survives a real perturbation to the underlying topology, rather than depending delicately on one specific and possibly flawed implementation.
C. Constellation Size Is Bounded by Geometry, Not Convenience
The infeasibility of small trial constellations (Section III.A) is a physical constraint on the problem rather than a limitation of our code. It deserves a short cautionary note in the final manuscript, since readers might otherwise assume constellation size is a free parameter to shrink for faster simulation.
D. Reporting Energy as a Ratio Rather Than an Absolute
Section III.D proves the headline percentage reduction is independent of the one energy model constant we cannot source. That is, in our view, the paper's single strongest methodological safeguard. It converts an admitted weakness, an unsourced FLOP count, into a result that does not depend on resolving that weakness at all.
VIII. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
•	Parameters were swept one at a time. Interaction effects between two or more parameters varied together are not captured by this design, and they remain for a future factorial study.
•	Real network traffic is self similar at every timescale. Our bursty model approximates that behaviour reasonably well, but it is not a full self similar generator. Producing one is future work.
•	The kNN regression is less sharp at the edges of its training distribution, meaning under very difficult conditions. Additional edge case data would sharpen its predictions there.
•	A routing path meant for polar regions, assumed to trigger past 70 degrees latitude, never activates at our 53 degree inclination. That code path is implemented correctly but sits dormant, ready for a future polar shell. We deliberately did not force it, since doing so would have manufactured an artificial effect.
•	The literature ultimately frames satellite energy through battery cycling and satellite lifetime rather than instantaneous joules. We model joules directly instead and leave the battery layer for future work.
•	The study uses a single flow and a single ground station pair. It assumes a circular orbit with no J2 perturbation or drag. Ground station link capacity and a laser terminal count of four are both fixed assumptions too. Each should be flagged as a simplifying choice, not an established fact.
•	The system currently reacts to the network's overall state rather than to a specific geographic region. A targeted response to a localized disaster or traffic hotspot would be a natural extension. It is not part of the present study.
IX. CONCLUSION
This paper introduced a trust gate for routing assisted by a digital twin in LEO satellite networks. It was motivated by an observation the prior literature has largely overlooked. A digital twin's recommendation is only as good as the telemetry it is built on, and that telemetry is never perfectly current. A fixed threshold gate captures much of the available benefit, but only at one operating point on the difficulty spectrum. An adaptive gate, selecting its own threshold from statistics the twin can itself observe, recovers the fixed gate's best case performance across the full spectrum without ever consulting ground truth. Across five seeds, 1500 ticks per run and two traffic regimes, the adaptive gate cut unnecessary route changes by roughly 80 percent and reconfiguration energy by roughly 78 percent relative to an ungated twin. Latency stayed close to an oracle bound throughout. We showed that this energy result holds regardless of a quantity we cannot presently source from the literature. We regard the trust gate as a general requirement for any autonomous control loop built on a digital twin, not as a feature specific to this one routing problem. That holds in satellite networks and elsewhere, wherever the twin's knowledge of the world it controls can lag the world itself.
